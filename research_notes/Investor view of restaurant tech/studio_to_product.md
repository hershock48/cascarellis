# Studio-to-product paths for a small local-business software studio (restaurant tech, embedded payments, Michigan), 2024–2026

Scope note: Researched 26 Oct 2026. Direct page fetches were blocked by the sandbox proxy (DNS/403 on inc.com, paymentsdive.com, stripe.com, michiganrise.com, investors.shift4.com, annarborusa.org), so most findings below come from search-result extracts of the cited pages rather than full-page reads. Where a figure appears in only one source, or is my own arithmetic from sourced inputs, it is marked as such. "Investor view" vs "founder report" is flagged where the distinction matters.

## Key Question 1: What do the documented studio-to-product transitions have in common, and what did investors or acquirers say the turning point was?

### Takeaway
Every well-documented studio-to-product story (37signals/Basecamp, Rocket Science Group/Mailchimp, Lightspeed, Toast, Lunchbox) followed the same arc: a product built to solve the founders' own or their clients' recurring problem, run alongside the services business for 3–7 years on client cash flow, then a deliberate shutdown of the services line before (or instead of) outside capital. The "turning point" acquirers and investors cite is never the agency history itself; it is the moment the product had its own customer base, recurring revenue and a reason to be bought (Bezos: profitable product, no need for money; Accel: 7 years self-funded, then $30M; Fiserv/BentoBox: 14,000 restaurant locations to fold into Clover).

### Cited Findings
- 37signals was founded in 1999 in Chicago as a web design consultancy; Basecamp was built from an internal project in 2004, Ruby on Rails was extracted from that work, and by 2005 the company "had moved away from consulting work to focus exclusively on its own web applications." — [Wikipedia: 37signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37signals)
- On 20 July 2006 Bezos Expeditions took a "minority private equity investment in 37signals"; the founders later described it as a minority, no-control stake, said the company "was profitable from the start, so none of the money went to fund growth," and that Bezos became an LLC member with a share of yearly profits and a one-time sell-back option that was never triggered. (Founder report.) — [Signal v. Noise, 2006](https://signalvnoise.com/archives2/bezos_expeditions_invests_in_37signals); [Signal v. Noise: The deal Jeff Bezos got on Basecamp](https://signalvnoise.com/svn3/the-deal-jeff-bezos-got-on-basecamp/)
- Jason Fried's stated reason for staying out of VC is independence: founders who raise "end up answering to someone else's schedule and return expectations"; 37signals today has fewer than 80 employees, over 100,000 customers, "tens of millions of dollars in profit each year, and no investors, board, or any plans to ever raise money or sell the company." (Founder report.) — [Practical Founders interview with Jason Fried](https://practicalfounders.com/articles/interview-jason-fried-ceo-of-37signals-and-author-of-rework); [Lenny's Newsletter: Jason Fried](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/jason-fried-challenges-your-thinking)
- Mailchimp began in 2001 as a side project of the Rocket Science Group, an Atlanta web design agency, "to meet a need their small-business clients had for email newsletters"; the agency stayed the main business for years, and around 2007 the founders shut the agency to focus on email marketing; Intuit agreed to buy Mailchimp for roughly $12B in 2021 with no outside funding ever raised. — [emailcloud timeline](https://www.emailcloud.com/timeline/mailchimp-12-billion/); [StackSync: The Bootstrapped Primate](https://www.stacksync.com/blog/the-bootstrapped-primate-the-origin-story-of-mailchimp); [inkl/Mailchimp side project](https://www.inkl.com/news/mailchimps-founders-turned-a-side-project-into-a-12-billion-business-without-outside-funding). Sources disagree on founding year (2000 vs 2001) and on whether Mark Armstrong was a co-founder ([Wikipedia: Mailchimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mailchimp)).
- Lightspeed (Montreal, founded 2005): founder Dax Dasilva says "in the first seven years, we bootstrapped the company"; the first outside investors were Accel, who "put in $30 million" in 2012; "we didn't spend a dollar of that for the first year," instead professionalizing the company, and spending began once the company committed to cloud/SaaS. (Founder report.) — [Research Money: Dax Dasilva Q&A](https://researchmoneyinc.com/article/scaleup-qa-lightspeed-ceo-dax-dasilva-on-building-a-growth-culture-through-shared-ownership); [Ivey Lightspeed profile](https://www.ivey.uwo.ca/scotiabank-digital-banking-lab/canada-fintech/payments-money-transfer/lightspeed/)
- Toast was founded (2011 or 2012; sources differ) by three ex-Endeca employees (Steve Fredette, Aman Narang, Jonathan Grimm) as a consumer app for paying/splitting restaurant checks that plugged into existing POS systems; the first customer, a Boston restaurant/pub, found the product unsatisfactory, legacy POS vendors would not integrate, and the team pivoted to building a cloud POS from the ground up. Early money is described as bootstrapping plus seed support from ex-Endeca executives, notably Steve Papa (one Medium post says $500,000 of Papa's own money; low-authority secondary sources). Toast signed its 1,000th customer in 2015. — [Entrepreneur: Scrappy origin story of Toast](https://www.entrepreneur.com/starting-a-business/how-toast-transformed-the-way-restaurants-do-business/474003); [Wikipedia: Toast, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toast,_Inc.); [Medium: Toast pivot](https://medium.com/@rohit_84770/toast-inc-pivot-growth-ipo-and-beyond-7b383a163394)
- Lunchbox: co-founder Nabeel Alamgir started as a busboy at Bareburger, rose to its first CMO, and "left in 2019 to found Lunchbox with his co-founders, Andrew Boryk and Hadi Rashid"; the idea came from seeing how many tech platforms an operator had to run and how much was paid in third-party delivery commissions. Bareburger is a Lunchbox customer, not the source of its code. Lunchbox raised a $20M Series A led by Coatue (Oct 2020) with 645 Ventures and Primary Ventures from the seed round, then a $50M Series B led by Coatue (Feb 2022); it charges a flat SaaS fee of about $200/month/location. — [BusinessWire: Lunchbox $20M Series A](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20201028005203/en/Lunchbox-the-Next-Gen-Restaurant-Ordering-Engine-Announces-Closing-of-its-20M-Series-A-Round); [Restaurant Business: Lunchbox raises $50M](https://restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/lunchbox-raises-50m-contributions-sweetgreen-doordash-execs); [Forbes profile: Nabeel Alamgir](https://www.forbes.com/profile/nabeel-alamgir/); [NRN: Lunchbox CEO](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-executives/lunchbox-ceo-nabeel-alamgir-wants-to-take-restaurant-branding-out-of-the-digital-dark-ages). Sources conflict on founding year (2018 vs 2019) and the co-founder list ([StartupIntros](https://startupintros.com/orgs/lunchbox-technologies); [Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/lunchbox/__lPIMkYYAhUuOap3jC8H62RujMiBHOIwS4SRMIhHRb0c)). I found no evidence that Lunchbox had "agency roots" in the sense of a dev shop; its roots are operator-side (a restaurant's marketing department).
- BentoBox (restaurant website design, online ordering and marketing tools, i.e. the closest analogue to a web studio that productized) was acquired by Fiserv for $317M net of $24M acquired cash, closed 22 Nov 2021, with ~7,500 restaurant "concepts" across 14,000 locations at announcement; Fiserv's stated rationale was to fold it into Clover, which serves some 200,000 restaurants. Purchase accounting: ~$52M identifiable intangibles (of which $25M acquired software/technology, $27M customer relationships and other), $266M goodwill. — [Fiserv 10-Q Q3 2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/798354/000079835422000019/fisv-20220930.htm); [Restaurant Dive: Fiserv/BentoBox](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/fiserv-expands-restaurant-reach-with-bentobox-purchase/608436/); [Restaurant Business: Fiserv signs deal to acquire BentoBox](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/payments-giant-fiserv-signs-deal-acquire-bentobox)
- A BugHerd case study documents an agency (Soul+Wolf, Melbourne) with a five-year plan to replace 80% of service revenue with product revenue; it is a plan in progress, not a completed transition. — [BugHerd: From services to SaaS](https://bugherd.com/blog/from-services-to-saas-inside-an-agencys-5-year-plan-to-replace-80-of-revenue-with-product)
- Olo (restaurant online-ordering SaaS, founded 2005, 750+ brands, 88,000 locations) was taken private by Thoma Bravo for ~$2.0B equity value ($10.25/share, a 65% premium), completed in 2025. Relevant as the top of the restaurant-ordering exit ladder, not a studio story. — [NRN: Olo to be acquired by Thoma Bravo](https://www.nrn.com/mergers-acquisitions/olo-to-be-acquired-by-software-investment-firm-thoma-bravo); [Verdict Foodservice](https://www.verdictfoodservice.com/news/thoma-bravo-acquisition-olo/)

### Inferences
- Common pattern: (1) the product is born from a repeated client need the studio kept re-solving; (2) services cash flow funds 3–7 years of product development with no outside money; (3) the founders eventually kill the services line (Mailchimp 2007, 37signals 2005) rather than run both indefinitely; (4) outside capital, when taken at all, arrives after the product is self-sustaining and is used for professionalization rather than survival (Lightspeed, 37signals).
- The purchase-price allocation in the BentoBox deal is the clearest acquirer statement of what was bought: only ~$25M of $317M was attributed to the software itself; the rest was customer relationships and goodwill (i.e., the installed base of 14,000 restaurant locations to push onto Clover payments). For a studio this implies the logo/location count and payments conversion opportunity, not the codebase, is what a processor pays for.
- None of the documented cases raised a priced round while still visibly an agency. The studio's realistic sequence is the Mailchimp/37signals one: productize, wind the services line down to a minority of revenue, then decide.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve direct quotes from Accel, Coatue or Bezos explaining their investment theses in their own words; the "turning point" statements above are founder-reported.
- I found no restaurant-tech company that is documented as having started as a client-services web/dev studio in the way Mailchimp and 37signals did; BentoBox is the closest in product (restaurant websites + ordering) but I did not find its origin story.
- Toast's early funding details are poorly sourced (secondary blogs); Toast's S-1 would be the primary source and was not fetched.

## Key Question 2: How do processors and payfacs value and acquire small ISVs with a payments stream, with examples and prices where reported?

### Takeaway
Processors buy restaurant/local-commerce software primarily for the merchant base they can convert to their own acquiring: Shift4's 2024–2025 deals (Revel $250M for ~18,000 locations; Givex ~$148M for 130,000+ locations; Focus POS $45M for ~10,000 restaurants) and Fiserv's BentoBox ($317M for 14,000 locations) price out to roughly $1,100–$23,000 per location depending on how much payment volume and software ARR comes with it (my arithmetic). Sub-$10M ISVs are not reported in deal press; the comparable market for them is residual-portfolio and sponsor-backed roll-up pricing, where disclosed multiples are scarce and stated on net revenue, EBITDA or monthly residuals.

### Cited Findings
- Shift4 agreed in May 2024 to buy Revel Systems for approximately $250.0M total purchase consideration, paid from cash on hand; Shift4's shareholder letter put Revel's POS in about 18,000 merchant locations representing a $17B payment opportunity; Shift4 planned to sell its payments to Revel's customers and roll Revel into SkyTab. — [Shift4 10-Q Q1 2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1794669/000179466924000016/four-20240331.htm); [Payments Dive: Shift4/Revel](https://paymentsdive.com/news/shift4-point-of-sale-revel-pos-merger-acquisition-payment/715958); [Restaurant Business: Shift4 to acquire Revel for $250M](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/payment-processor-shift4-acquire-revel-systems-250m)
- Shift4 bought Givex (gift card/loyalty/POS) for C$200M (~US$148M), closing Nov 2024, adding "more than 130,000 locations across more than 100 countries"; a secondary Substack says that by Q1 2025 Shift4 had cross-sold full acquiring to ~100 Givex merchants (single, unverified source). — [Digital Transactions: Shift4 lays out $148M for Givex](https://www.digitaltransactions.net/shift4-lays-out-148-million-in-cash-for-gift-card-platform-givexgsgsdfdsg/); [Payments Dive: Shift4/Givex](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/shift4-canadian-gift-card-company-givex-acquisition/725314/); [Bearstone: Shift4's acquisition playbook](https://bearstone2023.substack.com/p/shift4-acquisition-playbook)
- Shift4 bought Focus POS (restaurant POS, about 10,000 restaurant clients) for $45M in 2025, stating it would take on payment processing for those merchants and eventually migrate them to SkyTab. — [Payments Dive: Shift4 snags Focus POS for $45M](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/shift4-snags-focus-pos-for-45m-restaurant-point-of-sale-payments-earnings/649438/)
- Shift4's older playbook: it bought Restaurant Manager, POSitouch and Future POS in 2017–2018 and still runs those brands; in 2025 it agreed to buy Smartpay (AU/NZ, 40,000+ merchants) for NZ$296.4M (~US$180M) and closed Global Blue ($1.5B) in July 2025. — [Shift4 press release: three POS brands](https://www.shift4.com/news/shift4-payments-announces-acquisition-and-integration-of-three-leading-point-of-sale-brands); [Shift4 IR: Smartpay](https://investors.shift4.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/265/shift4-to-acquire-australian-payments-leader-smartpay); [Payments Dive: Global Blue](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/shift4-to-acquire-global-blue-for-15b/740355/)
- Fiserv/BentoBox: $317M net for ~7,500 concepts / 14,000 locations (see KQ1). — [Fiserv 10-Q Q3 2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/798354/000079835422000019/fisv-20220930.htm)
- Payroc "has been on an acquisition spree since about 2018, rolling up independent software vendors and other smaller players" and in 2024 agreed to buy i3 Verticals' merchant services business for $440M (not its software arm); in 2025 it bought PayiQ to own end-to-end processing and "enable rapid deployment for independent software vendors (ISVs), agents, referral partners and merchants." — [Payments Dive: Payroc preps for more acquisitions](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/payroc-acquisitions-ipo-card-processing-payments/720982/); [Parthenon Capital: Payroc/i3 Verticals](https://www.parthenoncapital.com/news/payroc-signs-an-agreement-with-i3-verticals-to-acquire-its-merchant-services-business/); [PYMNTS: Payroc to acquire PayIQ](https://www.pymnts.com/acquisitions/2025/payroc-to-complete-end-to-end-processing-platform-by-acquiring-payiq/)
- Stax's CEO says the company spent two years acquiring processing and settlement technology (including APPS) to own the full stack as "the foundational processing layer powering fintech offerings for ISVs, ISOs, and SMBs," and frames vertical SaaS platforms as partners that monetize through payments. — [Stax: acquires APPS](https://staxpayments.com/blog/stax-payments-acquires-apps/); [PYMNTS: Stax CEO on vertical SaaS](https://www.pymnts.com/partnerships/2025/vertical-saas-platforms-find-path-to-profits-with-partners-says-stax-ceo)
- A 2026 buyer's guide groups Stax, Payroc, Priority Technology and North American Bancard as sponsor-backed consolidators with "committed institutional capital for ISO and ISV acquisitions in the $1M to $25M EBITDA range"; it gives 12x–18x EBITDA for ISV/payfac businesses with proprietary software and sub-8% attrition, says the valuation "combines a SaaS multiple on the software ARR and a payments multiple on the embedded payment revenue," and puts stable ISO residual portfolios at 15x–22x monthly residual income. (Single advisory source; commercial interest.) — [CT Acquisitions: Buying a payment processing business in 2026](https://ctacquisitions.com/buy-a-business/payment-processing/)
- A 2026 payments valuation benchmark puts PayFac/merchant acquiring at 3.0x–6.0x EV/net revenue and PayFac-as-a-Service at ~8.0x, stating every multiple is "against net revenue … never payment volume." (Single advisory source.) — [Windsor Drake: Payments & PayFac valuation benchmarks 2026](https://windsordrake.com/payments-valuation-benchmarks/)
- The same advisor, citing Strawhecker Group H1 2026 data, says "disclosed multiples stay scarce, so headline comparables can mislead," and that "a processor prices merchant volume. A software acquirer prices attach rate." — [Windsor Drake: Who buys payments companies](https://windsordrake.com/who-buys-payments-companies/)
- Residual-portfolio pricing (trade press, undated): columnists cite low-20s to high-30s multiples of monthly residual, with outliers from 12x to 50x; buyers typically pay 70–80% at closing with the balance on an earn-out; a ~10% attrition guarantee is common; sellers who keep servicing merchants get "about four more multiples." — [Digital Transactions: How details influence merchant portfolio valuations](https://digitaltransactions.net/how-the-details-strongly-influence-merchant-portfolio-valuations/); [The Green Sheet, article 5887](https://greensheet.com/emagazine&article_id=5887); [The Green Sheet, article 6632](https://greensheet.com/emagazine&article_id=6632)
- Take-rate reference points: full PayFac registration 60–100+ bps, PayFac-as-a-Service 20–50 bps (vendor explainer). — [ISV Payment Integration: What is a PayFac](https://isvpaymentintegration.com/glossary/what-is-a-payfac/)
- Older but foundational: Adam Hark's 2016 columns argue markets assign higher multiples to technology/ISV companies than pure processors, and The Strawhecker Group's Peter Michaud said "technology and ISV integration is highly valued" in the context of GTCR's $260M purchase of Sage Payment Solutions. — [VAR Insights: ISV and VAR valuations are rising with payments integration](https://www.varinsights.com/doc/isv-and-var-valuations-are-rising-with-payments-integration-0001); [TSG: GTCR buys Sage Payment Solutions](https://tsgpayments.com/tsg-in-digital-transactions-in-yet-another-isv-play-gtcr-buys-sage-payment-solutions-for-260-million/)

### Inferences
- Price per location, my arithmetic from the sourced figures: Revel ≈ $13,900/location ($250M ÷ 18,000); BentoBox ≈ $22,600/location ($317M ÷ 14,000) or ≈ $42,000/concept; Focus POS ≈ $4,500/restaurant ($45M ÷ 10,000); Givex ≈ $1,100/location ($148M ÷ 130,000). The spread tracks how much processable volume and software ARR each location carries: a POS location with $1M/yr of card volume is worth an order of magnitude more to a processor than a gift-card endpoint.
- For a studio with ~20 client sites, 3 multi-tenant tenants and 5 ordering deployments, even the top of that range implies an acquisition value in the low six figures at most; processors at Shift4/Fiserv scale do not do deals that small. The realistic buyers at this size are sponsor-backed roll-ups (Payroc, Stax, North, Priority), regional ISOs buying residual streams, or a slightly larger restaurant ISV, and the pricing frame would be a multiple of monthly net payments residual plus a SaaS multiple on genuinely recurring fees.
- A flat $0.99 customer-paid order fee is transactional revenue, not a card-processing residual. Acquirers in the sources price (a) net payments revenue the ISV actually retains and (b) merchant volume they can re-price; a per-order convenience fee will likely be modeled as usage revenue with high churn sensitivity rather than as "payments revenue" unless the studio is the merchant-of-record or payfac sub-merchant owner on that volume.

### Gaps
- I found no reported 2024–2026 acquisitions by Global Payments, Nuvei, North or Payrix/Worldpay of a small restaurant or local-commerce ISV; coverage is dominated by large deals (Global Payments/Worldpay, Shift4). Payrix's acquisition activity could not be verified.
- No source gives a disclosed price for an ISV with fewer than ~10,000 merchants; the "per-merchant" figures above are my arithmetic on large deals and are not a small-deal benchmark.
- The Strawhecker Group's own ISV valuation data (H1 2026) is referenced second-hand only.

## Key Question 3: What are the realistic Michigan and Midwest sources of early capital for a vertical SaaS company with paying local customers, and what are their check sizes and criteria?

### Takeaway
Michigan has an unusually deep pre-seed layer for a company at this stage: Michigan Rise ($50K–$150K initial, up to $250K follow-on, requires matching capital and majority Michigan staff), ID Ventures' First Capital Fund (typically up to $150K initial, SAFE/convertible, milestone-tranched, aims to get companies to an angel/VC round within 12 months), the Michigan First Capital Fund ($50K–$150K), Michigan Angel Fund (~$250K–$2M, prefers at-or-near revenue), and Midwest seed funds like M25 (~$250K–$1M, vertical SaaS listed as a focus). Renaissance Venture Capital is a fund of funds, not a direct investor. Non-dilutive options are SBA microloans (max $50K) and revenue-based financing, which mostly starts at $15K–$50K MRR.

### Cited Findings
- Michigan Rise (Pre-Seed Fund III): initial investments $50,000–$150,000 with potential follow-on up to $250,000, via convertible debt, equity or SAFE; sectors include software and digital technologies; companies must be a for-profit LLC/C-Corp/S-Corp founded within the past seven years, with a majority of full-time staff in Michigan, and need matching investment from other funding sources. — [Michigan Rise: Investment criteria](https://michiganrise.com/criteria/); [Michigan Rise: Five investments, Jan 2026](https://michiganrise.com/2026/02/22/michigan-rise-announces-five-investments-in-january-2026/)
- ID Ventures (Invest Detroit) First Capital Fund: initial investment "typically up to $150K with follow-on opportunities" per the current funds page; older materials cite $75K–$150K average initial and "up to $250,000," so treat $150K as current. Criteria: key founders and at least one full-time employee in Michigan, an MVP created, a tech-enabled component, for-profit entity, an identified scalable venture path with "strong potential for future rounds of venture capital"; deals are convertible notes/SAFEs, released in tranches against milestones, aimed at reaching an angel/VC round within 12 months. — [ID Ventures: Our Funds](https://idventures.com/our-funds/); [Invest Detroit: Venture overview PDF](https://investdetroit.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Venture-Overview.pdf); [First Capital Fund PDF](https://innovatemarquette.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/First-Capital-Fund.pdf)
- Ann Arbor SPARK's startup-funding page lists the statewide Michigan First Capital Fund at $50,000–$150,000 alongside Michigan Rise. — [Ann Arbor SPARK: Funding for technology startup companies](https://annarborusa.org/spark-services/funding/startup-stage/)
- Michigan Angel Fund (Ann Arbor): most directories cite about $250K–$2M per company; it "prefers investing in early-stage start-ups that are at or near revenue." — [Caplink: Michigan Angel Fund](https://caplink.capital/investor-database/michigan-angel-fund); [Visible.vc: Michigan's startup scene](https://visible.vc/blog/top-vcs-in-michigan-startup-funding/)
- Grand Angels (West Michigan) is listed at $1.0M–$3.2M checks by one directory and $100K minimum by another; Detroit Venture Partners at $750K–$3.0M by one directory and a $250K "sweet spot" by another (aggregator data, inconsistent). — [Venture Capital Archive: Grand Angels Fund III](https://venturecapitalarchive.com/venture-funds/grand-angels-grandangels-org); [The Founders Group: Michigan investors](https://thefounders.group/investors/mi)
- M25 (Chicago, Midwest-only): check-size estimates across directories range $100K–$500K, $250K–$500K, $250K–$1M (with vertical SaaS listed as a focus) and up to $100K–$2M; Fund III was $31.8M. — [M25: Fund III announcement](https://www.m25vc.com/blog/m25-announces-318m-fund-iii-to-expand-funding-of-midwest-based-startups); [VCSheet: M25](https://www.vcsheet.com/fund/m25); [Visible.vc: Top Midwest VCs 2026](https://visible.vc/blog/us-midwest-top-vc-investors/)
- Renaissance Venture Capital Fund is a fund of funds that "does not make direct startup investments"; the MVCA profile lists a $4,000,000 preferred initial commitment (to funds), it closed a third fund with $81M, and Fund V received a Michigan Department of Treasury commitment in Feb 2025. — [Renaissance VC FAQ](https://renvcf.com/faq/who-is-renaissance-venture-capital/); [MVCA: Renaissance closes third fund](https://michiganvca.org/renaissance-81-million-fund/); [MVCA: Renaissance profile](https://michiganvca.org/investor/renaissance-venture-capital/)
- Michigan Capital Network (Grand Rapids, founded 2009 or 2012 depending on source) invests pre-seed through Series A; no check size found. — [PitchBook: Michigan Capital Network](https://pitchbook.com/profiles/investor/439049-62)
- MEDC/Michigan Strategic Fund: the Michigan Business Development Program gives performance-based grants/loans for job creation or capital investment; the state's Small Business Venture Capital Program is described as helping eligible startups access VC; the Michigan SBDC offers free consulting and lender packaging through 11 regional centers; Michigan Women Forward runs a $2,500–$50,000 microloan for Michigan-registered small businesses. — [Gusto: Business grants and loans in Michigan](https://gusto.com/resources/states/michigan/business-grants-and-loans); [Crestmont: Michigan small business loans guide](https://www.crestmontcapital.com/blog/small-business-loans-michigan-guide)
- SBA microloans: maximum $50,000, term no more than 6 years, rates negotiated with nonprofit intermediary lenders (typically 8–13% per one guide); the average microloan is about $13,000. SBA 7(a) maxes at $5M with the "7(a) Small" variant capped at $350,000; startups with under a year of history "may struggle to qualify." — [SBA lender resources](https://www.sba.gov/sba-lenders/); [Nav: SBA microloans](https://www.nav.com/business-financing-options/sba-microloans/); [Lendio: SBA loan maximums](https://www.lendio.com/blog/maximum-sba-loan-amounts); [Live Oak Bank: SBA loans in 2025](https://www.liveoak.bank/blog/sba-loans-in-2025-what-you-need-to-know)
- Revenue-based financing minimums (third-party and competitor pages, conflicting): Lighter Capital asks for about $15K MRR averaged over three months (≈$200K ARR) and lends up to ~33% of annualized run-rate, with repayment caps around 1.3x–1.5x; Capchase is cited anywhere from $100K ARR with 12 months history to $1M ARR, or $50K MRR with 6 months history, at a 6–12% flat discount fee; Pipe is cited at $100K–$200K ARR with a 5–10% trading discount. — [Capchase: Lighter Capital review](https://www.capchase.com/blog/lighter-capital-review); [Aventis Advisors: Top SaaS debt providers](https://aventis-advisors.com/top-saas-debt-funding-providers-for-founders/); [Elite Funders: Capchase vs Pipe vs Clearco](https://elitefunders.com/compare/capchase-vs-pipe-vs-clearco); [Founderpath: Revenue-based financing guide](https://founderpath.com/saas-financing/revenue-based-financing)

### Inferences
- The matching-capital requirement at Michigan Rise and the "path to a VC round within 12 months" framing at ID Ventures mean these funds are designed as the first institutional check before an angel/seed round, not as a substitute for one; a studio that intends to stay bootstrapped should not take them, because their covenants and tranche milestones presume a venture trajectory.
- At the studio's likely scale (low five figures of MRR across three tenants plus services), revenue-based financing is mostly out of reach until recurring product MRR reaches ~$15K–$50K; an SBA microloan via a Michigan intermediary, the SBDC, and client-funded development are the realistic non-dilutive sources this year.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch MEDC's own pages for the Small Business Venture Capital Program or current Michigan Rise/ID Ventures terms; figures are from search extracts of those sites and from aggregators.
- No check-size or criteria data found for Michigan Capital Network, Endeavor Detroit, TechTown's programs, Invest Michigan, or Ludlow Ventures.

## Key Question 4: What do seed investors in vertical SaaS state as minimum traction in 2025 to 2026, in ARR, logos and growth rate?

### Takeaway
Advisor and investor commentary converges on roughly $300K–$500K ARR with 10–20 paying customers for an institutional B2B software seed in 2025–2026, but one vertical-specific dataset shows much lower medians (construction $80K, healthcare $120K, legal $200K ARR; median 7 customers), with investors weighting 5–10 referenceable paying customers over the ARR figure. Market data: SVB puts 2025 seed-raisers at a $363K revenue base growing 322% YoY; Carta puts the 2025 median seed at ~$4.0M raised on ~$20M post-money (rising to $4.1M/$24.3M in the latest six-month cut).

### Cited Findings
- "The working expectation for institutional B2B software seed rounds now sits around $300K–$500K of ARR with ten to twenty paying customers," up from ~$200K two years earlier. (Advisor source.) — [SaaSRise: The 2026 SaaS funding guide](https://www.saasrise.com/blog/the-2026-saas-funding-guide)
- Stronger B2B SaaS seed rounds are "often in the range of $500,000 to $1 million or more" of ARR. (Advisor source.) — [Spectup: Series A traction metrics](https://www.spectup.com/resource-hub/series-a-traction-metrics)
- Vertical-SaaS-specific: an advisor's dataset of 83 founders who closed rounds in the prior 18 months shows construction-tech seeds at a median $80K ARR and 7 customers, healthcare $120K, legal $200K; ~60% of seed-closing founders had under $100K ARR; investors weighted 5–10 paying customers willing to take a reference call more heavily than ARR. (Single advisory source, small sample.) — [VCBoom: Vertical SaaS investors in 2026](https://www.vcboom.com/guides/vertical-saas-investors-2026)
- CRV (investor source) on what seed investors look for in B2B SaaS/AI: see its criteria page; summary emphasizes team, market and early proof over fixed ARR gates. — [CRV: What seed investors look for](https://www.crv.com/content/what-seed-investors-look-for); [CRV: B2B SaaS/AI investment criteria](https://www.crv.com/content/b2b-saas-ai-startup-investment-criteria)

- Solo-founder-specific bar: one guide recommends "$10k+ MRR for a seed round, since benchmarks are higher for solo founders." (Advisor source.) — [StartupFundraising.com: Raising VC as a solo founder](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/fundraising-strategies-for-solopreneurs-navigating-challenges-and-building-trust)

### Inferences
- For a restaurant/local-business product with three multi-tenant customers and five ordering deployments, the studio is below every stated seed threshold on ARR and logos; the Michigan pre-seed funds (KQ3) are the matching stage. The vertical-SaaS dataset suggests that 5–10 paying restaurants who will take a reference call, plus a measurable payments attach rate, would be a credible pre-seed story even at sub-$100K ARR.
- Investors will want ARR stated as product subscription plus the recurring portion of order fees, with services and custom-build revenue stripped out; the $0.99 fee stream will be judged on month-over-month order volume and retention, not as "ARR" unless it is contractually recurring.

## Key Question 5: How do investors react to a founder who is the sole developer, and what evidence changes their view?

### Takeaway
Investors and acquirers treat a bus factor of 1 as a pricing problem rather than an automatic no: Carta data shows solo-founder valuations nearly identical at Series A but slightly lower at priced seed, and acquirers respond with retention bonuses, earn-outs, escrow holdbacks or a discount. The evidence that changes their view is documentation sufficient for a stranger to deploy and operate the system, a credible second technical contributor (founding engineer, advisor or contractor), referenceable customers, and (for solo founders in 2025) a track record of shipping and revenue; solo founding has risen from 23% to 36% of new startups and AI tooling is the stated reason.

### Cited Findings
- Solo-founder fundraises in 2024 were "nearly identical to multi-founder companies at Series A, but slightly lower at the Priced Seed and Series B"; at priced seed "investors are mostly evaluating the founding team," so "a mild bias against solo founders likely pulls their median valuation down," while by Series A focus shifts to the business. — [SoloFounders.com: State of solo founding report](https://solofounders.com/report)
- Share of startups founded by a single entrepreneur rose from 23% in 2019 to 36% in H1 2025 (Carta data) and "nearly one-quarter of newly established startups" are solo-founded per an altshare report, with investors "requiring clearer milestones and greater business certainty before committing capital." — [SBC News: Solo founders, team AI](https://sbcnews.co.uk/features/2026/06/17/solo-founders-team-ai/); [Calcalist: altshare report](https://www.calcalistech.com/ctechnews/article/rknbfqmemx); [FourWeekMBA: Solo founders rise from 23.7% to 36.3%](https://fourweekmba.com/solo-founders-rise-from-23-7-to-36-3-ai-tools-enable-the-one-person-startup/)
- Example: Alisa Rae was rejected by Y Combinator as a solo founder, then raised a $2M pre-seed in 36 hours, onboarded a founding engineer, reapplied and was accepted. — [Capital Brief: All the single ladies](https://www.capitalbrief.com/newsletter/all-the-single-ladies-51895cd9-2835-44df-91fe-b33a6d0818b2/)
- Exit example: Base44, built by solo founder Maor Shlomo, was acquired by Wix for $80M in June 2025 after ~$1.5M revenue within a month of its Feb 2025 launch. — [Capital Brief](https://www.capitalbrief.com/newsletter/all-the-single-ladies-51895cd9-2835-44df-91fe-b33a6d0818b2/)
- "Investors now routinely check bus factor during due diligence. A company with bus factor 1 is harder to fund, harder to sell." — [FoundersBar: Bus factor in startups](https://foundersbar.com/articles-and-research/bus-factor-explained-silent-startup-killer)
- In M&A, "when the answer is 'one person holds it,' the deal does not usually die. It gets repriced. The standard mechanics are retention bonuses tied to earn-out periods, escrow arrangements that hold back part of the price until continuity is proven, and in blunt cases a straight discount." — [VeryCreatives: Bus factor explained](https://verycreatives.com/blog/bus-factor)
- Technical diligence red flags include "bus factor of 1 on revenue-critical systems," "more than 30% orphaned files," and "single contributors owning more than 40% of commits across the codebase"; buyers tie earn-outs to named individuals and set knowledge-transfer milestones such as identifying secondary contributors for critical systems. — [ContributorIQ: Software technical due diligence guide 2026](https://contributoriq.com/blog/technical-due-diligence-software-acquisition-guide); [CT Acquisitions: Technology due diligence in M&A](https://ctacquisitions.com/technology-due-diligence-in-mergers-and-acquisitions/)
- Acquire.com (buyer side): "one of the things we look for is technical documentation," recommending an onboarding manual for a new developer; a solo-developer checklist recommends five short documents covering env vars, deploy process, database access, third-party accounts and architecture overview, plus an inventory of credentials, deploy rights, vendor accounts and API keys the one person holds, with a rotation plan. — [Acquire.com: How buyers do technical diligence](https://blog.acquire.com/how-buyers-do-technical-diligence/); [Vibe Coder blog: Fixing your bus factor as a solo developer](https://blog.vibecoder.me/bus-factor-documenting-solo-project-for-others)
- Mitigations advised for solo founders raising: an advisory board of 3–5 operators spoken with every 2–4 weeks (advisor equity ~0.1–0.5%), and a higher traction bar ($10K+ MRR) than a team would need. — [StartupFundraising.com: Team slide for solo founders](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/how-to-present-the-team-slide-in-a-pitch-deck-when-team-is-non-existent); [StartupFundraising.com: Raising VC as a solo founder](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/fundraising-strategies-for-solopreneurs-navigating-challenges-and-building-trust)
- Founder-side reports of AI-assisted solo building: Fortune and DEV Community pieces describe solo founders using AI tooling to replace team functions; the "10–50x capital efficiency" claim and a Substack claim that 67% of 2024 pre-seed funding went to solo founders have no clear primary source and should be treated as unverified. — [Fortune: Solo founders, AI automation](https://dc.fortune.com/2026/05/18/solo-founders-ai-automation-entire-teams-entrepreneurs/); [DEV Community: How solo founders use AI](https://dev.to/insightlab/how-solo-founders-use-ai-to-compete-with-50-person-teams-n21)

### Inferences
- For a one-person studio with five separate client repos of the ordering/kitchen system, diligence metrics like "orphaned files" and "single contributor >40% of commits" will read as 100% concentration across the board; consolidating the five repos into the multi-tenant codebase is both a productization signal (KQ6) and the single largest bus-factor mitigation available.
- A part-time contractor who has actually deployed and fixed a production incident, plus the five-document runbook, is the cheapest credible answer to "what happens if you are hit by a bus"; a technical co-founder is the strongest answer for a raise but changes the cap table and is not required for an acquisition conversation.

### Gaps
- I found no source on how acquirers or investors treat AI-generated code provenance in 2025–2026 diligence (licensing, test coverage, maintainability); this is an open question.
- No systematic study of seed investors' treatment of sole-developer founders specifically (as opposed to solo founders generally) was found.

## Key Question 6: What should such a studio prepare in the next year to be ready for either a raise or an acquisition conversation, and what should it avoid doing?

### Takeaway
The sources agree on the preparation that serves both paths: separate product revenue from services in the books, show repeatable onboarding and one shared codebase, build a referenceable customer schedule with payments metrics (attach rate, volume, retention), keep a lightweight seed data room (a Drive folder is enough), and document the system so a second developer can run it. The things to avoid are mixing implementation fees into ARR, letting services exceed roughly a quarter of revenue if you want to be priced as software, raising Michigan pre-seed money if you do not intend a venture trajectory, and leaving the five client-specific repos unconsolidated.

### Cited Findings
- Services share: a secondhand summary of SaaStr podcast guidance says typical services revenue for enterprise SaaS is 8–10%, ~25% is still "normal for genuine enterprise deals," and "if services revenue creeps past about a quarter of the total, investors start pricing you as a services business, not a software one." (Secondary summary of investor commentary.) — [eChai Ventures: Will investors hate a services layer?](https://echai.ventures/startingup/business-models/how-do-i-decide-whether-to-add-a-services-layer-to-my-product-business-and-will-investors-hate-it)
- CFO/diligence sources: if services staff must grow to scale the business, pure SaaS investors see it as a red flag; heavy managed services "can make the company look more like an agency than a SaaS company," lowering valuation; investors discount material non-recurring revenue; bundling professional services or one-time implementation fees into subscription revenue "inflates recurring metrics" and loses investor confidence; buyers look for onboarding/support fees misclassified as recurring. One case study: implementation costs at 35% of revenue meant "it wasn't a scaling business; it was a services business." (Advisory sources with commercial interest; thresholds like 60–80% gross margin are rules of thumb.) — [The SaaS CFO: Revenue hierarchy](https://www.thesaascfo.com/the-saas-revenue-hierarchy-why-defining-your-revenue-streams-matter/); [CFO Pro Analytics: Financial red flags in early-stage SaaS](https://cfoproanalytics.com/cfo-wiki/saas/financial-red-flags-in-early-stage-saas-companies/); [ConsultEFC: SaaS due diligence red flags](https://consultefc.com/saas-due-diligence-red-flags-valuation/); [Ascent CFO: 5 financial red flags](https://ascentcfo.com/resources/5-financial-red-flags-that-drive-investors-away/)
- Mitigating point: well-managed professional services can increase stickiness and reduce churn; the recommended practice is to report services separately from ARR and show margins improving with scale. — [The SaaS CFO: Revenue hierarchy](https://www.thesaascfo.com/the-saas-revenue-hierarchy-why-defining-your-revenue-streams-matter/)
- Valuation gap commentary: SaaS businesses are valued on ARR multiples (~5x–10x in the post's estimate) while productized-service agencies are valued far lower; "your IP is worth more than billable." (Commentary.) — [David Hart Substack: Your IP is worth more than billable](https://davidhart.substack.com/p/your-ip-is-worth-more-than-billable)
- Seed data room: "A well-organized Drive folder with an index is enough for many seed diligences"; pre-seed/seed contents are pitch deck, cap table, incorporation documents, a 12–18 month milestone plan, basic P&L and burn rate; audited financials and multi-year projections are not expected. — [Papermark: Startup data room checklist 2026](https://www.papermark.com/blog/startup-data-room-checklist); [Matchplay Group: Seed round data room checklist](https://matchplaygroup.com/blog/seed-round-data-room-checklist-and-what-to-share-at-each-stage)
- Metrics: at seed investors want MRR growth, retention, early acquisition cost and pipeline quality; "investors compare deck numbers to exports," so definitions must match across deck and data room, and "if your ARR and growth numbers cannot be reproduced from the general ledger, the whole pitch is doubted." Customer schedule should separate unpaid evaluations from paying customers; references shared only with the customer's permission at seed, with 2–3 pre-briefed references expected by Series A. — [Shoutex: SaaS metrics investors want by stage](https://hub.shoutex.com/pitch-decks/saas-metrics-for-investors/); [CFO Matrix: SaaS data room diligence checklist](https://cfomatrix.in/insight/saas-finance/saas-data-room-diligence-checklist-skeletons); [Visible.vc: Startup data room](https://visible.vc/blog/startup-data-room/)
- Payments metrics investors anchor to: attach rate (share of active customers using embedded payments), GPV and take rate; "a strong take rate with a low attach rate doesn't move the needle." — [Bridges: Embedded payments for vertical SaaS](https://withbridges.com/guides/embedded-payments-vertical-saas); [Valpay: Economics of embedded payments](https://www.valpay.com/insights/the-economics-of-embedded-payments-for-vertical-saas)
- Processor pricing frame to prepare for: "a processor prices merchant volume. A software acquirer prices attach rate"; multiples are stated on net revenue, never volume; portfolio buyers pay 70–80% at close with the rest on earn-out and expect an attrition guarantee (~10%). — [Windsor Drake: Who buys payments companies](https://windsordrake.com/who-buys-payments-companies/); [Digital Transactions: Merchant portfolio valuations](https://digitaltransactions.net/how-the-details-strongly-influence-merchant-portfolio-valuations/)
- Bus-factor documentation (five documents: env vars, deploy process, database access, third-party accounts, architecture; credential inventory and rotation plan) and acquirer expectation of an onboarding manual. — [Vibe Coder blog: Fixing your bus factor](https://blog.vibecoder.me/bus-factor-documenting-solo-project-for-others); [Acquire.com: Technical diligence](https://blog.acquire.com/how-buyers-do-technical-diligence/)
- Michigan pre-seed funds require matching capital (Michigan Rise) and are explicitly milestone-tranched toward a VC round within 12 months (ID Ventures). — [Michigan Rise criteria](https://michiganrise.com/criteria/); [Invest Detroit venture overview](https://investdetroit.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Venture-Overview.pdf)
- Bootstrapper precedent for the "stay independent" option: 37signals (profitable, no board, no plans to sell) and Mailchimp (agency closed 2007, no outside money, $12B sale) show the services-funded path can end in either outcome. — [Practical Founders: Jason Fried](https://practicalfounders.com/articles/interview-jason-fried-ceo-of-37signals-and-author-of-rework); [emailcloud: Mailchimp timeline](https://www.emailcloud.com/timeline/mailchimp-12-billion/)

### Inferences
- Prepare (next 12 months), derived from the above: (1) a chart of accounts that splits subscription, order-fee/payments and services revenue, with ARR reproducible from the ledger; (2) migrate the five client-repo ordering/kitchen deployments onto the multi-tenant product so logo count (ideally 8–10+ restaurants) and "shared code" are literally true; (3) a per-merchant schedule with go-live date, monthly orders, GPV, order-fee revenue, Stripe/Square attach and churn, so attach rate and retention are computable; (4) a 20-slide deck and a Drive-folder data room per the seed checklists; (5) five runbook documents plus a contractor who has done a deploy; (6) a written register roadmap framed around payments attach, since every processor deal cited was priced on convertible merchant volume.
- Avoid: counting custom-build or implementation fees as recurring; letting services remain the majority of revenue if a software valuation is the goal; taking matching-required pre-seed money without a venture intent; building the register on a processor-locked stack before deciding whether the exit is to that processor; and letting the $0.99 fee be the only monetization, since acquirers in the sources price net payments revenue and software ARR, not convenience fees.
- The decision between raise, sell and bootstrap can be deferred for a year without penalty because the preparation list is identical for all three; what differs is only whether the studio signs a matching-required note (venture path) or an ISO/payfac referral agreement that gives it a residual stream (acquisition path).

### Gaps
- No source directly addresses how a customer-paid per-order fee (as opposed to a merchant-paid take rate) is valued by investors or processors; the inference above is mine.
- No first-party investor essay from a16z, Tidemark, Bessemer, Point Nine, First Round or SaaStr specifically on "agency dressed as a product" was retrieved; the services-share thresholds are from CFO advisors and a secondhand SaaStr summary.
- Stripe/Square partner-program economics (revenue share to ISVs, e.g., Square App Marketplace or Stripe partner rev-share) were not researched and would bear on the residual-stream path.
