# Restaurant Tech Buyers Pay for Merchant Volume

Venture investors and strategic acquirers evaluate a restaurant platform with embedded payments on a short list of things, and almost none of them is the code. Investors want to see a control point (the register and order flow), a payments attach rate above the vertical SaaS average of roughly **40 percent**, net revenue retention above 100 percent despite restaurant closures, software gross margin near 80 percent, and payments reported as net revenue after interchange and never as processing volume; Toast is the yardstick at about **$12,500 of ARR per location** (an inference from $2.05B ARR over 164,000 locations) and **98 basis points of total monetization on card volume**. Acquirers pay for the merchant book they can re-price, and the 2024 to 2026 deals price out at roughly **$1,100 to $25,000 per location** depending on how much card volume rides on each one, while venture multiples for the sector leader compressed from about **40x ARR at Toast's 2021 IPO to about 7x** in 2026 and payments-heavy POS books have changed hands at under 1x revenue. For Glazed Web, whose Jelly plan prices a restaurant at about $600 a month and caps its service model at "dozens" of restaurants, the honest reading is that the studio is below every stated seed threshold (about $300K to $500K ARR with 10 to 20 paying customers) and that its plan as written describes a profitable local business or an acquisition target, with the venture bar several times above its own stated ceiling; the per-location arithmetic puts its acquisition value today in the low six figures at most. Michigan has an unusually deep pre-seed layer (Michigan Rise, ID Ventures, the Michigan Angel Fund, M25 across the Midwest) but those funds require matching capital and tranche toward a venture round inside twelve months, so they suit a founder who has decided to raise and nobody else. Investors treat a sole developer as a pricing problem: the penalty is concentrated at priced seed, fades by Series A, and in a sale becomes an earn-out, an escrow holdback or a discount, all of which documentation and a second contributor reduce. The next year's preparation list is the same for a raise, a sale or staying independent: split subscription, payments margin, order fees and services in the books, consolidate the five client repos into the multi-tenant product, build a per-merchant schedule with attach, volume and retention, write the runbooks a stranger could deploy from, and keep the studio off any processor-locked rail before deciding who the buyer might be.

The consequence is that figures are reported as the search summary presented them, which is reliable for headline numbers in press releases and SEC exhibits and less reliable for context, definitions and dates. Where a number appeared in only one source it is marked single-source; where it came from a vendor with a commercial interest (Stripe, Adyen, Worldpay, Payrix, Payabli, the M&A advisory shops) it is marked as such; where the researchers computed a multiple or a per-location price from two sourced inputs the arithmetic is shown and labeled as an inference. Several figures conflict across sources and the conflicts are left visible: the Olo take-private is $2.0B in Thoma Bravo's release and $1.75B in one secondary summary, Carta's 2025 median seed round is $4.0M in one summary and $2.5M to $3.2M in another, Point Nine's Series A ARR range is far lower than Carta's median, and PitchBook's restaurant-tech funding peak is 2018 in one source and 2021 in another. Nothing here on the studio's own numbers comes from its books; the per-restaurant economics are the proposals in the Jelly plan, and the studio scale used for the arithmetic (about twenty client sites, three multi-tenant tenants and five ordering deployments) is the researchers' reading of the studio catalog.

## Investors fund a control point first and price payments on retention second

The published investor thesis has not changed since 2020 and every 2025 and 2026 benchmark restates it. Andreessen Horowitz's "Fintech Scales Vertical SaaS" argued that adding financial products to vertical software "can increase revenue per customer by 2-5x," that the wave "occurs first in vertical markets," and that the vertical SaaS vendor that best serves one industry often becomes dominant and can then sell financial products to that base ([a16z](https://a16z.com/fintech-scales-vertical-saas/)). The same essay distinguished a referral model, where the software company resells a processor, from an embedded model where it acts as payment facilitator with better economics, and said embedding "works best at scale, usually above $50M of gross merchandise value" ([a16z follow-up](https://a16z.com/2020/08/04/fintech-scales-vertical-saas-2/); single source, as extracted). Tidemark's framework is the one most benchmark publishers now use: a vertical SaaS company should "win their category, expand their offerings, and then, for the select few, extend through the value chain," and control points are "systems with data, workflow, and account gravity" that carry "the unfair right to sell multiple products" ([Tidemark podcast](https://theplatformjourney.tidemarkcap.com/episodes/25-dave-yuan-tidemark); [Tidemark VSKP](https://tidemarkcap.com/post/introducing-the-vertical-saas-knowledge-project)). A restaurant register with online ordering sits at the control point, payments is the first expansion product, and diner-facing ordering is already the extension to the customers' customers, so the Jelly design maps onto the thesis cleanly; the question investors ask is whether the studio can win a category, which in restaurants means visible share in a dense local market.

The retention argument is what gives payments its valuation weight. Tidemark's 2025 benchmark of more than 200 vertical SaaS companies found fintech-led companies have "the strongest retention profile," with gross revenue retention of **96 percent against roughly 90 to 91 percent** for the broader cohort, that 59 percent of vertical SaaS companies are multi-product, and that multi-product companies grew about 21 percent faster ([Tidemark 2025 report](https://tidemarkcap.com/vskp-chapter/2025-vertical-smb-saas-benchmark-report/); [SaaSletter summary](https://www.saasletter.com/p/vertical-saas-benchmarks-2025)). Tidemark's Andrew Walsh told Stripe Sessions 2025 that "across our entire dataset, we have an average attach rate of about 40%. That's up from 27% last year," and that hospitality attaches far above industrial trades ([Stripe Sessions 2025](https://stripe.com/sessions/2025/how-to-benchmark-your-vertical-saas-platform)). Stripe's 2026 recap, a vendor publication, claims platforms with embedded financial products see 11 percent lower annual churn and multiproduct platforms grow 49 percent faster, and attributes to Tidemark an estimate that each customer adopting embedded payments adds about **$4,200 of incremental ARR** ([Stripe Sessions 2026 blog](https://stripe.com/blog/vertical-saas-insights-sessions-2026); single source for the $4,200 figure). The 2024 Tidemark survey of 246 vendors set the medians a seed or Series A investor will compare against: ARR growth 36 percent, GRR 88 percent, NRR 108 percent ([Tidemark 2024 survey PDF](https://www.cfodesk.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/2024-Vertical-SMB-SaaS-Benchmarking-Survey.pdf)). The 2025 medians for growth and retention were not published in any accessible form, so the 2024 figures stand in.

Bessemer's language has moved. Its 2024 State of the Cloud named "Vertical AI dwarfs legacy vertical SaaS" as one of five trends and described the report as "a eulogy for the legacy cloud" ([WisdomTree summary](https://www.wisdomtree.com/ch/insights/blog/state-of-the-cloud-bvps-five-ai-trends-for-2024/pdf); [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/legacy-cloud-evolving-vertical-ai-dholakia-bessemer)). The 2025 and 2026 editions were not retrievable, but the deal flow confirms the shift: the restaurant rounds that cleared in 2025 and 2026 went to companies with an AI narrative and disclosed nine-figure ARR, and a 2026 pitch for a restaurant platform will be read partly on whether it changes restaurant labor economics, beyond software plus payments. Bessemer's Eric Kaplan, quoted in an Adyen report, adds the warning that platforms competing on price end up "in a race to the bottom" and that go-to-market execution now matters more than technology ([Adyen embedded payments report](https://www.adyen.com/campaigns/uk/embedded-payments-report); vendor-hosted). Point Nine's shorter list still governs seed: "Team, PMF, user love, TAM, CAC payback, retention, and moats are still the name of the game" ([Point Nine](https://writing.pointnine.com/p/what-does-it-take-to-raise-capital-87b)).

At fiscal 2025 close it had about **164,000 locations**, **$195.1B of gross payment volume**, ARR above **$2.0B** (the 10-K gives $2,047M), revenue of $6,153M, and a CEO claim of "Adjusted EBITDA margins of 34%" ([Toast FY2025 release](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260212058106/en/Toast-Announces-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Financial-Results); [Toast FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1650164/000165016426000057/tost-20251231.htm)). Two things about Toast's accounting matter for a small company. First, Toast defines ARR as subscription revenue plus fintech gross profit, annualized, and its blended GAAP gross margin is only about 27 percent because payments and hardware are low-margin; the 34 percent EBITDA margin is a margin on recurring gross profit, and the researchers' arithmetic puts it near 10 percent of total revenue ([multiples.vc Toast](https://multiples.vc/public-comps/toast-valuation-multiples)). Second, Toast's own 10-K lists reliance on "a limited number of payment processors" and processor-linked outages that "have occurred before, and similar events may recur" as risk factors, which is the same single-rail question a seed investor will put to a studio building on Stripe Connect ([Toast FY2025 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1650164/000165016426000057/tost-20251231.htm)). Olo is the software-only reference: Q2 2025 revenue of $85.7M, about 89,000 locations, ARPU near $955 per location per quarter (about $3,800 a year), NRR of 114 percent and gross margin of 51 percent ([Olo Q2 2025 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1431695/000143169525000022/oloq22025exhibit991.htm)).

## Payments are valued on gross profit and the 2021 multiples are gone

Investors value embedded payments on net revenue after interchange and network fees, never on processing volume, and the public comps are built that way. Toast's ARR counts fintech gross profit; Shift4 reports "gross revenue less network fees" and defines its spread as the yield "after network fees" ([Shift4 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1794669/000179466925000007/ex991q4.htm); [MarketBeat Shift4 Q4 2025 call](https://www.marketbeat.com/instant-alerts/shift4-payments-q4-earnings-call-highlights-2026-02-28/)). The consequence shows in the headline multiples. Toast trades at about **2.2x to 2.8x trailing revenue** (multiples.vc lists 2.2x as of October 2026; market caps of $15.75B to $17.18B over about $6.15B trailing revenue give 2.6x to 2.8x by the researchers' arithmetic) and at roughly 7x annualized gross profit ([multiples.vc Toast](https://multiples.vc/public-comps/toast-valuation-multiples); [Macrotrends](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/TOST/toast/market-cap); [CompaniesMarketCap](https://companiesmarketcap.com/toast/marketcap/)). Shift4 is listed at about 3.1x revenue and 6.7x EBITDA, with the caveat that the listing's fields look internally inconsistent ([multiples.vc Shift4](https://multiples.vc/public-comps/shift4-payments-valuation-multiples)). Block's roughly $46B market cap against $12.51B of 2026 gross profit guidance is about 3.7x gross profit ([CompaniesMarketCap Block](https://companiesmarketcap.com/square/marketcap/); [Investing.com Block Q2 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/block-q2-2026-slides-record-27-margins-stock-falls-5-93CH-4839800)). Against those, pure software still carries a premium: Windsor Drake's Q4 2025 report puts premium vertical SaaS at a **7.8x median EV/revenue** against 5.3x to 6.1x for public horizontal SaaS, and SaaSRise's 2026 report puts vertical SaaS buyout multiples at 5.8x against 4.1x horizontal ([Windsor Drake Q4 2025](https://windsordrake.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Vertical-SaaS-Valuation-Report.pdf); [SaaSRise 2026](https://www.saasrise.com/blog/vertical-saas-ma-vc-report-2026); both advisory sources). The same advisors claim vertical SaaS with embedded fintech commanded a 33 to 60 percent premium over software-only peers, a single-source and unaudited figure. The broad market has compressed underneath all of this: SEG's public SaaS index median fell from 5.7x EV/TTM revenue in Q2 2025 to **3.2x in Q2 2026**, and private SaaS M&A ran at a 4.0x median with vertical software taking 54 percent of SaaS deals ([SEG 2Q26 via SandHill](https://sandhill.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/SEG-Research-2Q26-SaaS-MA-and-Public-Market-Report.pdf)). Gross margin is the swing factor: SEG's Q2 2025 cohorts traded at 7.2x for gross margin above 80 percent and 3.5x below 60 percent ([Orb compilation of SEG data](https://www.withorb.com/blog/saas-growth-margin-statistics); secondary).

The deal record from 2021 to 2026 shows what that compression did to restaurant tech specifically. Toast priced its IPO in September 2021 at about $20B on $494M of ARR, roughly 40x, with 47,942 locations ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/09/21/toast-sells-shares-at-40-in-ipo-topping-expected-range.html); [Toast S-1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1650164/000119312521258447/d166297ds1.htm)); by autumn 2026 it trades at about 7x ARR, a compression of five to six times by the researchers' arithmetic. Olo listed at a $3.55B market cap in March 2021 and was taken private by Thoma Bravo in September 2025 for about **$2.0B equity value** at $10.25 a share, a 65 percent premium to the unaffected price, despite revenue roughly doubling in between ([Thoma Bravo release](https://www.thomabravo.com/press-releases/thoma-bravo-completes-acquisition-of-olo); [PYMNTS on the IPO](https://www.pymnts.com/news/ipo/2021/olos-common-stock-will-go-for-25-per-share/)). On 2024 revenue of $285M that is about 7.0x trailing revenue; SaaSRise lists the deal at about 6.2x on a run-rate basis, and one secondary source reports the price as $1.75B, a conflict left unresolved ([SaaStr on Olo](https://www.saastr.com/pe-loves-saas-again-thoma-bravo-buys-olo-for-2-billion/); [Panabee](https://www.panabee.com/news/olo-goes-private-in-1-75-billion-thoma-bravo-acquisition-at-10-25-per-share)). The venture side has one outlier: Owner.com raised $120M at $1B in May 2025 and then $240M at **$2.3B** in August 2026 led by Goldman Sachs Alternatives after passing $100M ARR, which is up to about 23x ARR by the researchers' arithmetic and an upper bound because the ARR figure is a floor ([Restaurant Business on the Series C](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/tech-supplier-ownercom-raises-120m-giving-it-1b-valuation); [SiliconANGLE on the Series D](https://siliconangle.com/2026/08/28/owner-raises-240m-for-its-restaurant-management-platform/)). Critics note Owner does not disclose gross margin, NRR or CAC payback ([Signals Inbox](https://signalsinbox.com/blogs/news/vertical-saas-owner-overvalued)).

The counterexamples (Owner.com, Restaurant365) are software-led with disclosed nine-figure ARR. The second pattern is that payments-heavy POS books are priced on gross profit or per location, never on gross revenue: Upserve's up-to-$81M on $140M of payments-heavy revenue is the clearest case, and the BentoBox purchase accounting is the clearest statement of what a processor thinks it is buying, with only about **$25M of the $317M** attributed to acquired software and the balance to customer relationships and goodwill, meaning the 14,000 locations to push onto Clover payments ([Fiserv 10-Q Q3 2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/798354/000079835422000019/fisv-20220930.htm)). An advisory source summarizes the two buyer lenses in one line: "a processor prices merchant volume. A software acquirer prices attach rate," and every multiple is stated "against net revenue … never payment volume" ([Windsor Drake, who buys payments companies](https://windsordrake.com/who-buys-payments-companies/); [Windsor Drake payments benchmarks](https://windsordrake.com/payments-valuation-benchmarks/); single advisory source). The same advisor puts PayFac and merchant acquiring at 3.0x to 6.0x EV/net revenue and PayFac-as-a-service near 8x, and a buyer's guide for sponsor-backed consolidators gives 12x to 18x EBITDA for ISV or payfac businesses with proprietary software and under 8 percent attrition, stating that the valuation "combines a SaaS multiple on the software ARR and a payments multiple on the embedded payment revenue" ([CT Acquisitions](https://ctacquisitions.com/buy-a-business/payment-processing/); single source with commercial interest).

## Four buyer groups are active and each pays for something different

The buyers of restaurant technology since 2023 fall into four groups, and Toast and Block are in none of them. Toast's last acquisition was Delphi for $10M in February 2023 and searches of its 8-K filings and newsroom found nothing in 2025 or 2026; Block's last was GoParrot in 2022 ([Toast 10-Q Q2 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001650164/000165016426000164/tost-20260630.htm); [Square press release](https://squareup.com/us/en/press/square-acquires-goparrot)). Lightspeed is now a seller in US hospitality, and searches found no restaurant-tech acquisitions by Global Payments, NCR Voyix or Oracle in 2023 to 2026.

The first group is payments processors buying POS for the merchant book. Shift4 is the clearest and most active: Focus POS, Revel, Vectron, the Appetize unit, Givex, Smartpay and Global Blue between 2023 and 2025, each with the stated plan to migrate merchants onto SkyTab and cross-sell Shift4 acquiring ([Payments Dive on Revel](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/shift4-point-of-sale-revel-pos-merger-acquisition-payment/715958/); [Shift4 10-K FY2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1794669/000179466926000010/four-20251231.htm)). Fiserv's BentoBox deal was explicitly to extend Clover's roughly 200,000 restaurant clients, and Fiserv says Clover's US restaurant GPV grew at four times the industry rate from 2023 to 2025 ([Yahoo on Fiserv and BentoBox](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/fiserv-acquires-bentobox-expand-digital-100759581.html); [TIKR on Fiserv Q4 2025](https://www.tikr.com/blog/fiserv-q4-2025-earnings-eps-beat-revenue-flat-first-half-headwinds-ahead); secondary). Below that tier sit sponsor-backed roll-ups: Payroc "has been on an acquisition spree since about 2018, rolling up independent software vendors," bought i3 Verticals' merchant services business for $440M in 2024 and PayiQ in 2025 to "enable rapid deployment for independent software vendors"; Stax spent two years buying processing and settlement technology to be "the foundational processing layer" for ISVs ([Payments Dive on Payroc](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/payroc-acquisitions-ipo-card-processing-payments/720982/); [PYMNTS on Stax](https://www.pymnts.com/partnerships/2025/vertical-saas-platforms-find-path-to-profits-with-partners-says-stax-ceo)). A buyer's guide groups Stax, Payroc, Priority Technology and North American Bancard as holding "committed institutional capital for ISO and ISV acquisitions in the $1M to $25M EBITDA range" ([CT Acquisitions](https://ctacquisitions.com/buy-a-business/payment-processing/)). Residual portfolios, the smallest unit a processor buys, trade at low-20s to high-30s multiples of monthly residual income with outliers from 12x to 50x, 70 to 80 percent paid at close and the rest on earn-out, and a roughly 10 percent attrition guarantee ([Digital Transactions on portfolio valuations](https://digitaltransactions.net/how-the-details-strongly-influence-merchant-portfolio-valuations/); [The Green Sheet](https://greensheet.com/emagazine&article_id=5887)).

The second group is platform strategics buying a feature or a niche their customers already use. Olo bought Wisely, Omnivore and (under Thoma Bravo, December 2025) Spendgo because about 65 percent of Olo locations already used a loyalty tool ([BusinessWire Spendgo](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251222826702/en/Olo-Completes-Acquisition-of-Spendgo-to-Expand-Platform-with-Loyalty-Solution)); DoorDash bought Bbot and SevenRooms; PAR bought Stuzo for about $190M and Delaget for a $132M headline, mostly in stock ([PAR 10-K FY2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/708821/000070882125000015/par-20241231.htm)); Square bought GoParrot because it was already an integrated partner; ChowNow bought Cuboh, which had raised only about $1.4M; GoTab bought opsi; Lunchbox bought Spread and NovaDine ([Verdict on Cuboh](https://www.verdictfoodservice.com/news/chownow-acquires-restaurant-tech-cuboh/); [GoTab on opsi](https://gotab.com/latest/gotab-acquires-opsi-to-transform-restaurant-efficiency-and-profitability)). The recurring rationale is that the target was a top integration partner, which the researchers read as a visible path to acquisition for a small vendor. The third group is card networks buying dining distribution: American Express assembled Resy, Tock, Rooam and TheFork into roughly 75,000 bookable venues ([Amex newsroom](https://www.americanexpress.com/en-us/newsroom/articles/travel-and-dining/to-enhance-dining-platform--american-express-enters-agreement-to.html)). The fourth is permanent-capital consolidators and private equity buying cash-flowing or shrinking vertical software cheaply: Constellation's Harris (TouchBistro) and Volaris (Zonal, one of 14 vertical-software acquisitions in H1 2026), Skyview (Upserve), Corsair (HungerRush and its pizza add-ons Menufy, 9Fold and OrdrAI), and Thoma Bravo (Olo) ([Volaris mid-year update](https://www.volarisgroup.com/acquired-knowledge/volaris-groups-2026-mid-year-acquisition-update/); [HungerRush](https://www.hungerrush.com/restaurant-industry-news/featured-in-the-deal-hungerrush-and-the-great-restaurant-tech-catchup/)).

The gap that matters for a small studio is at the bottom of the size range. No disclosed purchase price was found for any 2023 to 2026 acquisition of a US restaurant-tech company with confirmed revenue under $10M; Cuboh, opsi, Spendgo, Pilot POS, Rooam, GoParrot, xtraCHEF and Sling were all undisclosed. The disclosed small deals (Delphi $10M, Focus POS $45M, Omnivore $49M, Bbot about $88M) suggest a $10M to $100M band for a feature or niche buy with a real customer base, and nothing below it is visible. No bar, brewery or coffee POS acquisition surfaced (Arryved remains independent), and no Michigan or Midwest restaurant POS acquisition surfaced at all. Trade analysis in mid-2025 said consolidation was accelerating "as investors favor scale over specialization," and a January 2026 piece expected consolidation to define 2026, naming kitchen automation, labor optimization, guest engagement and multi-location management as the likely target categories ([Restaurant Technology News, July 2025](https://restauranttechnologynews.com/2025/07/restaurant-technology-consolidation-accelerates-as-investors-favor-scale-over-specialization/); [Restaurant Technology News, Jan 2026](https://restauranttechnologynews.com/2026/01/how-restaurant-tech-ma-is-regaining-momentum-and-what-it-means-for-2026/)).

The venture backdrop is thin at the early stage. Restaurant-tech VC fell from nearly $12B globally in 2021 (PitchBook, via Restaurant Business) to about **$1.3B in 2024**, with one source dating the peak to 2018 at $14.5B, a definitional conflict ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/why-investors-are-pouring-billions-restaurant-technology); [The Spoon](https://thespoon.tech/report-restaurant-tech-funding-drops-to-1-3b-in-2024-but-ai-automation-provide-glimmer-of-hope/)). Dollars recovered in 2025 through a few large rounds and buyouts while counts kept falling; H1 2026 foodtech VC was $3.6B across 359 deals against $4B across 493 a year earlier, and the Q2 2026 deal count was an eight-year low with rounds "fewer, larger, and almost entirely late stage" ([PitchBook Q2 2026 first look](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q2-2026-agrifood-vc-first-look); [Private Markets Insights](https://www.privatemarketsinsights.com/post/foodtech-stopped-trying-to-invent-food-and-started-trying-to-run-it)). Tracxn's narrower restaurant SaaS category counted $146M of equity across 25 rounds through July 2026 ([Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/sectors/restaurant-saas/__hIeUshhrzVDyGjUDz_1tSxjcsXn_nn5vBOzgFs16xdg)). Layoffs at Lunchbox, ChowNow, 7shifts, Lightspeed and others, and the TouchBistro wipeout, are the other side of the same market. A small studio should expect a thinner early-stage market than 2021 and more interest from strategics and consolidators than from venture funds.

## Seed needs roughly 100 locations and Series A roughly 400, by inference

The stage bars are general B2B SaaS numbers because no restaurant-specific or vertical-SaaS-with-payments stage data exists. Pre-seed in 2025 is a sub-$500K round that is almost always a SAFE, with median post-money caps near $10M for $250K to $1M rounds and $15M for $1M to $2.5M rounds ([Kruze citing Carta](https://kruzeconsulting.com/preseed-funding/)). Seed is a round of about **$4.0M on roughly $20M post-money** per Carta's 2025 median, rising to $4.1M on $24.3M in its latest six-month software cut, the highest seed valuation Carta has recorded; a SaaS guide attributes a lower $2.5M to $3.2M round size to Carta, a conflict ([Carta H2 2025 benchmarks](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-fundraising-benchmarks-h2-2025/); [Metal.so](https://www.metal.so/collections/us-saas-seed-round-benchmarks-2025-average-round-size-valuations-dilution)). On traction, advisors converge on **$300K to $500K ARR with ten to twenty paying customers** for an institutional B2B software seed, up from about $200K two years earlier, and SVB's data puts 2025 seed-raisers at a $363K revenue base growing 322 percent year over year ([SaaSRise 2026 funding guide](https://www.saasrise.com/blog/the-2026-saas-funding-guide); [MEAN CEO citing SVB](https://blog.mean.ceo/?p=5720)). One vertical-specific dataset of 83 founders, small and from a single advisor, shows much lower medians (construction $80K ARR and seven customers, healthcare $120K, legal $200K), with about 60 percent of seed closers under $100K ARR and investors weighting five to ten referenceable paying customers over the ARR figure ([VCBoom](https://www.vcboom.com/guides/vertical-saas-investors-2026)). Series A clustered at a **$3M median ARR for SaaS in 2024 with a 75th percentile near $7M**, ARR at A roughly doubled since 2021 while valuations rose only modestly, and Carta says some investors "now expect companies to be closer to $5 million or even $10 million in ARR" ([Carta on $7M ARR Series A](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-7m-arr-series-a-saas-2025/); [Carta Series A hard market](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-series-a-metrics-saas-hard-market/)). Point Nine's 2025 napkin, seen only in a secondary summary, puts Series A at $0.5M to $2.5M ARR with 2x to 3x growth, and its 2023 napkin said $1M to $5M, so the Series A bar conflicts across sources by a factor of two or more ([Nuoptima summary](https://nuoptima.com/saas-requirements-benchmark-raising-capital); [Point Nine 2023 napkin](https://medium.com/point-nine-news/what-does-it-take-to-raise-capital-in-saas-in-2023-56d8f617714)). Median Series A pre-money in 2025 was $41.2M for non-AI companies against $57.5M for AI ([Carta AI vs non-AI](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-series-a-benchmarks-ai-vs-non-ai-2025/)). Seed investors also want a burn multiple "comfortably under 1.5x" ([SaaStock](https://saastock.com/blog/saas-vc-fundraising); advisor source).

Translating those ARR bars into restaurant locations is the researchers' inference and the arithmetic is as follows. Toast's ARR per location is about $12,500 ($2.05B over 164,000), of which the majority is fintech gross profit including Toast Capital; a startup without capital products and with a thinner take would run lower, perhaps $5,000 to $8,000 per location. On that range, a $1M ARR seed-stage proof point is about **100 to 200 paying locations with payments attached**, and a $3M Series A is about **400 to 600 locations**. Using the Jelly plan's own proposed economics the picture is tighter: the plan models about $600 a month per restaurant ($160 in platform fees, $200 in processing margin, $250 in order fees on $80,000 of monthly card volume and 500 online orders), or about $7,200 a year, and only the first two lines (about $4,300 a year) would count as ARR under the Toast convention of subscription plus payments gross profit. At $4,300 the $500K seed bar is about 115 locations and the $3M Series A bar is about 700; at the full $7,200 it is about 70 and 420. Either way the seed bar is several times the "dozens" the plan says its service model can support, which is the central tension examined below. For a one-person studio the fundability threshold the sources describe is paying customers plus a repeatable acquisition motion before a priced seed; given Toast's density argument, the restaurant-specific version is visible share in one metro or town, and the vertical dataset suggests five to ten referenceable paying restaurants plus a measurable attach rate would be a credible pre-seed story even under $100K ARR.

### A sole developer costs valuation points that documentation buys back

The evidence on solo founders is more favorable than founders usually assume, and it is specific about where the penalty sits. Carta data summarized by a solo-founder report shows solo-founder valuations in 2024 "nearly identical to multi-founder companies at Series A, but slightly lower at the Priced Seed," because at seed "investors are mostly evaluating the founding team" and by A the business drives the decision ([SoloFounders report](https://solofounders.com/report)). CRV's page says the same: the gap "narrows by Series A," and "reaching meaningful traction before the first priced round largely removes the solo-founder penalty," with a cited peer-reviewed study of 3,526 startups finding solo founders more likely to keep ventures running ([CRV on solo founders](https://www.crv.com/content/solo-founder)). The share of new startups with a single founder rose from 23 percent in 2019 to 36 percent in H1 2025, with AI tooling the stated reason ([FourWeekMBA on Carta data](https://fourweekmba.com/solo-founders-rise-from-23-7-to-36-3-ai-tools-enable-the-one-person-startup/)). A vendor dataset puts solo founders at about 20 percent of VC-funded deals against 37 percent for two-founder teams and describes the lone founder as "a single point of failure in all areas" ([Eqvista](https://eqvista.com/solo-founder-fundraising/); unverified). The sole-developer case is sharper than the sole-founder case, because technical diligence guides list "bus factor of 1 on revenue-critical systems" and "single contributors owning more than 40% of commits" as red flags and tie earn-outs to named individuals with knowledge-transfer milestones ([ContributorIQ](https://contributoriq.com/blog/technical-due-diligence-software-acquisition-guide)). In a sale the usual result is repricing: "the deal does not usually die. It gets repriced," through retention bonuses tied to earn-out periods, escrow holdbacks until continuity is proven, or a straight discount ([VeryCreatives](https://verycreatives.com/blog/bus-factor)). What changes the view is documentation a stranger could deploy from (Acquire.com's buyer team says "one of the things we look for is technical documentation"), a second technical contributor who has actually done a deploy, referenceable customers, and for a raise an advisory board of three to five operators and a somewhat higher traction bar, one guide suggesting $10K or more of MRR ([Acquire.com](https://blog.acquire.com/how-buyers-do-technical-diligence/); [StartupFundraising.com](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/fundraising-strategies-for-solopreneurs-navigating-challenges-and-building-trust)). The exit precedent is Base44, a solo-founder product acquired by Wix for $80M in June 2025 four months after launch ([Capital Brief](https://www.capitalbrief.com/newsletter/all-the-single-ladies-51895cd9-2835-44df-91fe-b33a6d0818b2/)). For Glazed Web, whose ordering and kitchen system exists as five separate client repos plus the DeVine payment engine and the Scooplist multi-tenant product, every commit-concentration metric would read as 100 percent today, and consolidating those repos into the multi-tenant codebase is both the productization signal and the largest bus-factor mitigation available.

## Jelly's plan reads to an investor as a bootstrap or acquisition story

Read through the lenses above, the studio's own plan tells a diligence team most of what it needs, and the parts that cut against a venture raise are deliberate design choices. The Jelly plan prices a location at $99 a month plus $29 a terminal, processing at Stripe's rate plus a stated 0.25 percent platform fee, a 99 cent customer-paid order fee split in half with the house, hardware at cost, and a service promise of a named person within five minutes during service hours, and it says plainly that this "scales to the number of restaurants one studio can know by name, which is dozens, and that is the business" (Jelly platform proposal and plan, sections 7 and 8). The plan also computes its own benchmark: Toast earns about $5,000 a year of gross profit per million dollars of card volume at its 48 to 49 basis point net take ([Toast Q3 2025 prepared remarks](https://s28.q4cdn.com/141746709/files/doc_financials/2025/q3/Q3-25-Prepared-Remarks.pdf)), and Jelly lands near a third of that per restaurant by design, because the gap is what the operator keeps and is the reason to switch. An investor reads the "dozens" ceiling and the deliberately thin take together and concludes the plan is built to be a profitable local business, in the 37signals and Mailchimp tradition, and that is a legitimate outcome: 37signals took only a minority, no-control Bezos stake in 2006 after it was already profitable, and Mailchimp shut its Atlanta agency around 2007, never raised, and sold to Intuit for about $12B in 2021 ([Signal v. Noise](https://signalvnoise.com/archives2/bezos_expeditions_invests_in_37signals); [emailcloud Mailchimp timeline](https://www.emailcloud.com/timeline/mailchimp-12-billion/)). None of the documented studio-to-product transitions (37signals, Mailchimp, Lightspeed's seven bootstrapped years before Accel's $30M) raised a priced round while still visibly an agency ([Research Money on Dasilva](https://researchmoneyinc.com/article/scaleup-qa-lightspeed-ceo-dax-dasilva-on-building-a-growth-culture-through-shared-ownership)).

The fee model is the first thing a diligence team would re-cut. The flat 99 cent customer-paid order fee is transactional revenue, and no source found addresses how investors or processors value a customer-paid per-order fee as opposed to a merchant-paid take rate; the researchers' inference, which this report adopts, is that it will be modeled as usage revenue with high churn sensitivity rather than as "payments revenue," unless the studio is the merchant of record or sub-merchant owner on that volume. In the Jelly economics the order fee is the largest of the three lines ($250 of the $600), so the studio's "ARR" on the Toast convention is about 60 percent of its product revenue. The 0.25 percent platform fee on card volume is the line that does count, because it is net revenue the studio retains through Stripe Connect's application fee on volume it has placed, and it is the Jelly equivalent of Toast's 48 to 50 basis point net take. It is also small in investor terms: 25 basis points sits at the top of the referral band and the bottom of the integrated band in the Payrix model (referral 0 to 20 bps, integrated up to about 40, PayFac 60 to 100), and a16z's $50M GMV threshold for embedding is roughly 50 Jelly restaurants at $80,000 a month ([Payrix 101 PDF](https://get.payrix.com/hubfs/Whitepapers/Payrix_Payment_Facilitation_101.pdf); [a16z follow-up](https://a16z.com/2020/08/04/fintech-scales-vertical-saas-2/)). The plan's own note that "interchange-plus with Stripe is a negotiation at volume and belongs on the two-year roadmap" is the honest version of that constraint. Stripe and Square partner economics (revenue share to ISVs through Stripe partner programs or the Square App Marketplace) were not researched and are a gap that bears directly on whether the processing margin line can grow without a payfac registration.

The second thing diligence re-cuts is the services line. A secondhand summary of SaaStr guidance says that "if services revenue creeps past about a quarter of the total, investors start pricing you as a services business, not a software one," and CFO advisors say bundling implementation or custom-build fees into subscription "inflates recurring metrics" and that heavy managed services "can make the company look more like an agency than a SaaS company" ([eChai Ventures](https://echai.ventures/startingup/business-models/how-do-i-decide-whether-to-add-a-services-layer-to-my-product-business-and-will-investors-hate-it); [CFO Pro Analytics](https://cfoproanalytics.com/cfo-wiki/saas/financial-red-flags-in-early-stage-saas-companies/); [The SaaS CFO](https://www.thesaascfo.com/the-saas-revenue-hierarchy-why-defining-your-revenue-streams-matter/)). Tom Tunguz's data has most professional services at zero or negative gross margin and says services above half of revenue "substantially depress overall gross margin" ([Tom Tunguz](https://www.tomtunguz.com/gross-margin-cashflow-margin-trends-in-saas/)). Glazed Web today is a studio whose revenue is mostly custom builds and site fees, and the Jelly plan adds onboarding "as a general contractor," hardware installs, a quarterly visit and the five-minute promise. Those are the right product decisions for a Homer pizza tavern and they are exactly what the 70 percent gross margin line flags if they are billed as product revenue. The mitigation the sources give sits in the accounting: report subscription, payments margin, order fees, hardware and services as separate lines, keep hardware at cost as pass-through, and show services margin improving as the second and third restaurants onboard without new code.

The third is the productization gate, and here the plan and the investor view agree exactly. The Phase 3 gate in the Jelly plan is "the second restaurant onboarded without a line of code written for it," which the plan calls the Scooplist test and "what makes this a product." That is the same test every diligence checklist applies when it asks whether revenue is spread across customers and whether the product is embedded in workflow rather than built per client ([BigIdeasDB diligence checklist](https://bigideasdb.com/saas-acquisition-due-diligence-checklist)). Until Phase 3 passes, an investor sees five ordering deployments in five repos and a workroom in three copies, which is a custom-software history with a shared component registry, and the registry is the asset to show because it is the evidence that the sixth restaurant is a pin and needs no port. The rail decision also reads well: the plan chooses Stripe Terminal because Square's Terminal API lacks offline mode and refuses to collect the platform fee from the same account, keeps DeVine on Square as a second live adapter, and ships a local hub for offline operation. Toast's own 10-K treats processor dependence as a disclosed risk, and a seed investor will ask the same question of a Stripe-only register; a second working adapter and a payment engine with an abstracted attempt state machine are the answer.

The channel is the fourth. Toast closes about 45 percent of deals with an on-site visit against 15 percent without, and one in five of its deals comes from a referral ([SaaStr on Toast's CRO](https://www.saastr.com/the-top-10-strategies-toasts-cro-uses-to-crush-quotas)). The Jelly plan concedes it gets only the referral channel, with a visible restaurant in Homer running well as the referral. Investors who fund restaurant platforms fund territory density, and Toast says it still gains share in metros above 30 percent penetration; a referral-only motion is consistent with a profitable local business and inconsistent with the 2x to 3x annual growth a Series A expects. If the studio ever wants the venture path, the thing it would have to show is that a second town's density came from something repeatable, which means a documented acquisition motion with a cost per location and a payback period, the two numbers Kyle Poyar's 2025 benchmark of 800 companies found most predictive of growth alongside NRR ([Growth Unhinged 2025 benchmarks](https://kylepoyar.substack.com/p/2025-saas-benchmarks-report)).

What is the studio worth to a buyer today? The per-location yardsticks from the deals table run from about $1,100 (Givex endpoints with no card volume) through $4,500 (Focus POS), $6,250 (TouchBistro, shrinking), $13,900 (Revel) and $22,600 (BentoBox) to a ceiling near $25,000 (Upserve, mostly earn-out), and they scale with how much processable volume and software ARR each location carries. Applied to a studio with about twenty client sites, three multi-tenant tenants and five ordering deployments, even the top of that range gives an acquisition value in the low six figures at most, and processors at Shift4 or Fiserv scale do not do deals that small; the realistic buyers at this size are sponsor-backed roll-ups (Payroc, Stax, North, Priority), regional ISOs buying residual streams, or a slightly larger restaurant ISV such as a GoTab, ChowNow or Arryved filling a gap, with pricing framed as a multiple of monthly net payments residual plus a SaaS multiple on genuinely recurring fees. Those per-location figures are the researchers' arithmetic on deals of 3,200 to 130,000 locations and are not a small-deal benchmark; no disclosed price exists for an ISV under roughly 10,000 merchants. The practical reading is that an acquisition conversation becomes worth having at the point where Jelly has a few dozen restaurants with Stripe Connect volume and a residual stream a buyer can model, which is the same point the Jelly plan calls "a business."

## Michigan's pre-seed layer is deep and assumes a venture path

Michigan has more institutional pre-seed money for a company at this stage than most states, and all of it is designed as the first check before an angel or seed round rather than as a substitute for one. Michigan Rise's Pre-Seed Fund III makes initial investments of **$50,000 to $150,000** with follow-on up to $250,000 by convertible debt, equity or SAFE, requires a for-profit entity founded within the past seven years with a majority of full-time staff in Michigan, and requires **matching investment** from other sources ([Michigan Rise criteria](https://michiganrise.com/criteria/)). ID Ventures' First Capital Fund (Invest Detroit) invests "typically up to $150K with follow-on opportunities," by convertible note or SAFE released in tranches against milestones, for companies with key founders and at least one full-time employee in Michigan, an MVP, and "strong potential for future rounds of venture capital," with the explicit aim of reaching an angel or VC round within twelve months ([ID Ventures funds](https://idventures.com/our-funds/); [Invest Detroit venture overview PDF](https://investdetroit.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Venture-Overview.pdf)). Ann Arbor SPARK lists the statewide Michigan First Capital Fund at $50,000 to $150,000 ([Ann Arbor SPARK](https://annarborusa.org/spark-services/funding/startup-stage/)). The Michigan Angel Fund in Ann Arbor is cited at about $250K to $2M per company and "prefers investing in early-stage start-ups that are at or near revenue" ([Caplink](https://caplink.capital/investor-database/michigan-angel-fund)). Directory figures for Grand Angels (West Michigan) and Detroit Venture Partners conflict badly ($100K minimum versus $1.0M to $3.2M checks; a $250K "sweet spot" versus $750K to $3.0M) and should be confirmed directly ([Venture Capital Archive](https://venturecapitalarchive.com/venture-funds/grand-angels-grandangels-org); [The Founders Group](https://thefounders.group/investors/mi)). Across the Midwest, Chicago's M25 invests only in Midwest companies, lists vertical SaaS as a focus, and is cited anywhere from $100K to $1M a check on a $31.8M third fund ([M25 Fund III](https://www.m25vc.com/blog/m25-announces-318m-fund-iii-to-expand-funding-of-midwest-based-startups); [Visible.vc Midwest VCs](https://visible.vc/blog/us-midwest-top-vc-investors/)). Renaissance Venture Capital Fund, often named in Michigan lists, is a fund of funds that "does not make direct startup investments" ([Renaissance FAQ](https://renvcf.com/faq/who-is-renaissance-venture-capital/)). No check size or criteria surfaced for Michigan Capital Network, Endeavor Detroit, TechTown, Invest Michigan or Ludlow Ventures, and nothing on Michigan or Midwest investor appetite for restaurant tech specifically was found.

The matching-capital requirement and the twelve-month path to a venture round are the reason a studio that intends to stay independent should leave this money alone: the covenants and tranche milestones presume a trajectory the Jelly plan does not describe. Non-dilutive money is thin at this scale. SBA microloans cap at $50,000 and the 7(a) Small variant at $350,000, with startups under a year of history likely to struggle to qualify ([Lendio](https://www.lendio.com/blog/maximum-sba-loan-amounts); [Live Oak Bank](https://www.liveoak.bank/blog/sba-loans-in-2025-what-you-need-to-know)). Revenue-based lenders mostly start at $15K to $50K of recurring MRR with six to twelve months of history, which the three multi-tenant tenants and five ordering deployments do not yet produce; at the studio's likely scale the realistic non-dilutive sources this year are an SBA microloan through a Michigan intermediary, the free Michigan SBDC consulting and lender packaging, and client-funded development, which is the engine the Jelly plan already runs on. MEDC's Business Development Program grants are performance-based on job creation or capital investment and the state's Small Business Venture Capital Program could not be read directly ([Gusto on Michigan programs](https://gusto.com/resources/states/michigan/business-grants-and-loans)); both are worth a direct inquiry once the hub hardware and a second hire make the studio a capital-investment or job-creation story.

## One preparation list serves a raise, a sale and staying independent

The useful finding across all three notes is that the next twelve months of preparation are identical whichever door the studio eventually opens, so the decision can be deferred a year without penalty; what differs is only whether the studio later signs a matching-required note or a referral and residual agreement. The first job is the books. Set up a chart of accounts that separates subscription (the $99 and $29 lines), payments margin (the 0.25 percent application fee, net), order fees (the studio's half of the 99 cents), hardware at cost, and services (builds, onboarding, installs, visits), and make ARR reproducible from the ledger, because "investors compare deck numbers to exports" and "if your ARR and growth numbers cannot be reproduced from the general ledger, the whole pitch is doubted" ([CFO Matrix](https://cfomatrix.in/insight/saas-finance/saas-data-room-diligence-checklist-skeletons); [Shoutex](https://hub.shoutex.com/pitch-decks/saas-metrics-for-investors/)). The second is the per-merchant schedule: one row per restaurant with go-live date, monthly orders, card volume through Stripe Connect, order-fee revenue, which rail it is on, and churn, so that attach rate, GPV retention and NRR are computable by cohort. Investors in this vertical look at GPV retention and NRR ahead of raw logo churn, because closures make 20 to 30 percent annual logo churn normal, and Toast's NRR above 100 percent comes from location expansion and module attach ([SaaStr on SMB churn](https://www.saastr.com/the-challenge-with-smb-saas-high-growth-can-only-mask-high-churn-for-just-so-long/)). Embedded-payments advisors add that "a strong take rate with a low attach rate doesn't move the needle," so the schedule should make attach visible on day one ([Bridges](https://withbridges.com/guides/embedded-payments-vertical-saas)). The third is the codebase: migrate the five client-repo ordering and kitchen deployments onto the multi-tenant product the Jelly plan's Phase 3 describes, so that the logo count (ideally eight to ten or more restaurants) and "shared code" are literally true, and so that commit concentration stops reading as a bus factor of one across five repositories. The fourth is the runbook set the acquirer side asks for: environment variables, deploy process, database access, third-party accounts and architecture, plus an inventory of every credential, deploy right, vendor account and API key the one person holds and a rotation plan, with a part-time contractor who has actually done a production deploy and fixed an incident ([Vibe Coder blog](https://blog.vibecoder.me/bus-factor-documenting-solo-project-for-others); [Acquire.com](https://blog.acquire.com/how-buyers-do-technical-diligence/)). The fifth is a lightweight data room, since "a well-organized Drive folder with an index is enough for many seed diligences": deck, cap table, incorporation documents, a twelve to eighteen month milestone plan (the Jelly phases and gates already are one), basic P&L and burn, with the restaurant-specific additions the generic checklists omit, namely location-level cohort retention, attach and net take in basis points, the software versus payments margin split, the Stripe Connect agreement and PCI scope, and hardware economics ([Papermark](https://www.papermark.com/blog/startup-data-room-checklist); [Klehr Harrison](https://klehr.com/publications/startup-evolution-a-year-long-journey-through-a-founders-toolkit-due-diligence-ready-building-a-data-room-that-wins-investor-confidence/)). The sixth is a written register roadmap framed around payments attach, since every processor deal in the record was priced on convertible merchant volume. The seventh is to keep three to five restaurant operators as an informal advisory circle spoken with every few weeks, which is the cheapest answer to the team question at seed and which the plan's quarterly visit already half-does.

The things to avoid follow from the same sources. Do not count custom builds, onboarding or hardware installs as recurring revenue, and do not let services remain the majority of revenue if a software valuation is ever the goal; the quarter-of-revenue line is where pricing flips. Do not take Michigan Rise or ID Ventures money without a venture intent, because the matching requirement and the twelve-month tranche milestones presume one. Do not build the register on a processor-locked stack before deciding whether the exit is to that processor; the Stripe Terminal choice with Square kept live at DeVine is the right shape, and a payfac or referral agreement that locks volume to one acquirer should wait until a buyer is in view. Do not let the 99 cent fee be the only monetization, since buyers price net payments revenue and software ARR and no source values a customer-paid convenience fee at all. Do not raise on a "we beat Toast's rate" claim, which the Jelly plan already rules out, because Bessemer's warning about competing on price is the one most often quoted back at restaurant platforms, and the plan's actual pitch (a stated markup, no term, hardware the operator owns, a person who answers) is the better story for an investor as well as an operator. Do not present the plan's thin take as a weakness to be fixed; present it as a deliberate wedge with a path to 40 to 60 basis points at volume through interchange-plus and a PayFac-as-a-service arrangement, which is the only version of the payments line an investor will model as growing. And do not skip the Phase 3 gate for the sake of location count: a sixth restaurant onboarded with new code is worth less to every buyer in this report than a third onboarded with none.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question the studio should be asking. Whether Glazed Web is "fundable" is the wrong frame for the next year, because the plan it has written is a profitable-local-business plan with a product gate inside it, and both the venture record (Owner.com, Restaurant365) and the acquisition record (BentoBox, Revel, Focus POS) reward the same thing at the point of transaction: a book of restaurants whose card volume runs through the platform and whose retention can be shown by cohort. Nothing in the deal record pays for a codebase, a component registry or an elegant offline hub, and the BentoBox purchase accounting put a number on that at about 8 percent of price. The studio's leverage therefore comes from the moment the Scooplist test passes for a register, because that is when its per-restaurant economics stop being a services estimate and become a cohort.

Two implications follow. The first is that the thin, honest take the Jelly plan chooses as its wedge is also the thing that caps its venture story, and the studio should decide consciously, after Phase 2, whether the wedge is permanent or an entry price that rises with volume through interchange-plus and a managed payfac; the sources suggest only the second version attracts a growth investor, while the first is perfectly sufficient for a Payroc-style roll-up or a GoTab-style strategic. The second is that the sole-developer question is less a barrier than a price, and the price falls fastest through boring work: separated books, one repository, five runbooks and a contractor who has deployed. A year from now the studio with those four things and ten restaurants on one codebase can choose among a matching-required Michigan note, a residual-and-earn-out conversation with a processor, or no outside capital at all, and will be the only party in the room who knows which it wants.
